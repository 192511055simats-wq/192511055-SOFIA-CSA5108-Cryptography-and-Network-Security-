01 / INTRO

What Blowfish Is

Blowfish is a symmetric-key block cipher published by Bruce Schneier in 1993 as a fast, free, unpatented replacement for aging ciphers like DES. It was built with a specific brief: run efficiently on the 32-bit processors of the era, keep the design simple enough to audit, and give developers a licence-free algorithm they could drop into any product.

Rather than a fixed key length, Blowfish lets the key size flex, and rather than a novel structure, it leans on a well-studied one — the Feistel network — while spending its design effort on generating a large, key-dependent set of substitution tables before a single byte of plaintext is touched.

02 / DIMENSIONS

Block Size & Variable Key Size

Blowfish fixes the data dimension but leaves the secret dimension open, so implementations can trade setup cost against brute-force resistance.

Fixed

64-bit Block

Every plaintext block is split into two 32-bit halves, L and R, which travel through the Feistel rounds together. A 64-bit block was standard for its era but is the cipher's best-known weak point today — see Limitations.

32–448-bit Key

The key can be anywhere from 32 to 448 bits, in 8-bit increments, letting a designer pick the security margin. The key itself is never used directly on data — it only seeds the key schedule that builds the P-array and S-boxes.

03 / ARCHITECTURE

Working Architecture

Blowfish's design splits cleanly into two phases: an expensive, one-time key schedule, and a cheap, repeatable encryption routine that reuses whatever the schedule produced.

Key Expansion

The secret key is used to repeatedly encrypt an all-zero block, and the output overwrites, in sequence, an 18-entry P-array and four 256-entry S-boxes (each holding 32-bit values). This takes roughly the effort of encrypting 4 KB of data and only needs to happen once per key.

Data Encryption

Once the P-array and S-boxes exist, each 64-bit block passes through 16 rounds of a Feistel network that reads from those tables. This phase is fast and is what runs per-block during actual use.

04 / STRUCTURE

The Feistel Network

Each round splits the block, scrambles one half with a function derived from the key material, and swaps — a structure that guarantees decryption is just encryption run with the subkeys in reverse.

In every round: the left half L is XORed with a round subkey from the P-array, then fed into function F. F splits the resulting 32 bits into four 8-bit bytes, sends each byte through its own S-box, then combines the four lookups with addition and XOR to produce a new 32-bit value. That value is XORed into the right half R. L and R then swap places before the next round begins.

After round 16, the final left/right swap is undone (it would otherwise be pointless — the classic Feistel finishing touch), and the two halves are each XORed with the last two entries of the P-array, P17 and P18. No published attack breaks the full 16-round cipher faster than brute force; reduced-round variants (down to around 4 rounds) have been analysed and broken in cryptanalysis literature, which is part of why the full 16 rounds are considered the minimum, not a conservative buffer.

06 / TRADE-OFFS

Advantages & Limitations

Blowfish's profile is a direct product of its two-phase design: cheap per-block encryption bought with an expensive, memory-hungry setup.

Advantages

  • Fast block encryption on 32-bit hardware once the key schedule is built
  • Free, unpatented, and open for any use — no licensing barrier to adoption
  • Simple, well-documented Feistel structure that's easy to implement and audit
  • Variable key length lets implementers tune the security margin
  • Strong avalanche effect: a one-bit change cascades across the output
  • No practical break of the full 16-round cipher after three decades of scrutiny

Limitations

  • 64-bit block size is vulnerable to birthday-bound collision attacks (e.g. SWEET32) on long-lived, high-volume connections under one key
  • Expensive key schedule (~4 KB of computation) makes frequent key changes costly
  • Large memory footprint for P-array and S-box storage — a poor fit for constrained embedded devices
  • Superseded in new designs by its own successor, Twofish, and by AES
  • A small class of mathematically "weak keys" exists, though rare in practice
07 / DEPLOYMENT

Applications

Blowfish's free licensing got it embedded widely through the 1990s and 2000s; much of that footprint persists in legacy systems today.

01

Password hashing

The bcrypt hashing scheme is built directly on Blowfish's expensive key schedule, repurposing its cost as a brute-force deterrent.

02

VPN & secure shell

Offered as a selectable cipher in early OpenSSH, OpenVPN, and various legacy VPN implementations.

03

Disk & file encryption

Included as an option in numerous file- and disk-encryption utilities from the 1990s–2000s era.

04

Embedded & legacy systems

Low implementation cost made it a common choice in older embedded devices and firmware.

05

Cryptographic libraries

Bundled in widely used libraries (e.g. OpenSSL, Libgcrypt) for backward compatibility with existing deployments.

06

Teaching cryptography

Its clean Feistel structure makes it a standard teaching example for how block ciphers are built.

STEP 1

Key schedule already generated the P-array and four S-boxes (one-time cost per key).

STEP 2

Plaintext block is split into L0 and R0, two 32-bit halves.

STEP 3

16 rounds run: XOR with Pᵢ, pass through F, XOR into the other half, swap.

STEP 4

The final round's swap is undone, restoring the correct L/R order.

STEP 5

Halves are XORed with P17 and P18, the last two subkeys.

STEP 6

The two results are recombined into the 64-bit ciphertext block.
